# Operating Principles

These are the principles that guide how you operate. Not aspirations — actual operating rules you've tested and trust.

Review these quarterly. Edit freely. Add what's missing. Remove what no longer fits.

---

## Decision-Making

### 1. Default to action over analysis

Most decisions are reversible. The cost of waiting is usually higher than the cost of being wrong. When in doubt, run a small experiment.

### 2. Optimize for energy, not time

Time is finite. Energy is renewable but depletable. Protect high-energy activities. Eliminate energy drains aggressively.

### 3. Apply the regret minimization framework

When facing a major decision: "When I'm 80, looking back, will I regret not doing this?" Useful for career pivots, relationship decisions, and adventures.

### 4. Distinguish between high-stakes and low-stakes decisions

High-stakes: Hire slowly, gather data, sleep on it.
Low-stakes: Decide in under 2 minutes, never revisit.

### 5. Say no by default

Protect your calendar, attention, and commitments. The opportunity cost of yes is always another yes you can't make.

---

## Work Philosophy

### 6. Leverage over effort

Ask: "What's the highest-leverage thing I could be doing right now?" If you're doing low-leverage work, either delegate it, automate it, or stop doing it.

### 7. Deep work is sacred

Protect 2-4 hours daily for uninterrupted thinking and creation. This is not optional. It's where your actual value is created.

### 8. Meetings are expensive

The true cost of a meeting = (number of people) × (average hourly rate) × (hours) + (context-switching cost for each person). Calculate this. Decline more meetings.

### 9. Context switching is poison

Batch similar tasks. Batch meetings. Protect transitions. The switching cost is real and cumulative.

### 10. Rest is productive

Recovery is not the absence of work. It's the foundation of work. Sleep, exercise, and downtime are non-negotiable.

---

## Relationships

### 11. Hire slow, fire fast (but fair)

The cost of a bad hire compounds. The cost of not exiting a bad hire compounds faster. Move quickly once you know.

### 12. Give direct feedback immediately

The longer you wait, the more unfair it becomes. Feedback within 24 hours is a kindness.

### 13. Build your personal board of directors

Maintain 3-5 trusted advisors you can call with hard problems. Cultivate these relationships intentionally.

### 14. Your reputation is your leverage

It takes years to build and moments to destroy. Make the long-term choice even when it's costly short-term.

---

## Self-Management

### 15. Know your high-energy windows

When do you do your best thinking? Protect those hours ruthlessly. Stack low-energy work in low-energy windows.

### 16. Physical health is a business strategy

Sleep, movement, and nutrition directly affect your judgment and stamina. Treat them as strategic investments.

### 17. Avoid reactive mode

The inbox is someone else's priorities. Start your day with your priorities before opening email or Slack.

### 18. Create forcing functions

Systems beat intentions. Deadlines, coaches, commitments — use external structures to ensure you do what matters.

### 19. Regular reflection is non-negotiable

This entire system exists because regular reflection prevents drift, catches problems early, and compounds wisdom.

---

## Life Philosophy

### 20. Play long-term games with long-term people

Optimize for relationships and work that compound over decades.

### 21. Be bored sometimes

Creativity and insight require white space. If every moment is filled, you're not thinking — you're reacting.

### 22. Money is a tool, not a score

Past a certain point, more money doesn't increase happiness. Know your number. Optimize for freedom, not accumulation.

### 23. Define your own success

Society's definition of success may not match yours. Be explicit about what you're optimizing for.

### 24. Remember you will die

Not morbid — clarifying. Limited time makes prioritization obvious. What would you stop doing if you had one year left?

- Deep Work vs. Leadership Presence: Your team needs access to you. Find the balance.
